# H3 Shot Planning

Write executable visual development, not a list of camera adjectives. Use the caller's existing content plan and H3 output format; do not add planning schemas, model calls or review gates.

## Preserve intent; develop the open space

User-specified actions, order, timing, framing, reference roles and prohibitions remain authoritative. A supplied opening is not necessarily a complete storyboard. For incomplete generation requests, develop purposeful actions, reactions and reveals in the remaining time without changing locked facts or outcomes. A local edit, by contrast, preserves untouched source action and camera behavior; it is not permission to redesign the film.

Resolve contradictory requirements using the caller's conflict policy, not by silently dropping one. Do not promote a mood word, uncertain perception or optional cinematic idea into a mandatory effect.

## Decide what changes before deciding where to cut

Distinguish an editorial cut from a new action phase, camera move or on-screen transformation:

- Fixed interfaces, continuous performances and local video edits can contain many events in one shot. Describe their ordered progression, not one shot per event.
- In a montage or narrative, cut when the next view changes what the viewer can understand or feel: a reveal, reaction, spatial relationship, scale, time/place, product function, or motivated rhythmic accent. Honor explicitly requested cuts even if another treatment is possible.
- Returning to the same person or subject can advance an exchange or musical rhythm. Remove redundant coverage, not purposeful repetition. A different angle alone does not require another shot.

Choose the structure from the task, not a default shot count. Do not stretch a brief opening pose across an otherwise open film; do not fragment a continuous dance into decorative coverage.

## Use each reference in its actual role

Identity sheets, scene boards, style references, action phases and chronological storyboards are different inputs. Multiple panels may supply one character, one world or several connected views. Multiple pictures may belong to one continuous shot. When the user requests the supplied storyboard's shot sequence and rhythm, preserve its separate views and order rather than merging them for economy. Otherwise picture count alone does not determine shot count.

Place a keyframe where its action state belongs: first frame, intermediate beat, last frame, or composition reference. Do not start from the completed result when the user asks to show its arrival. Preserve the selected scene's recognizable anchors without carrying unrequested panel borders or unrelated identities into the frame.

For video reference, inherit only requested dimensions: choreography, performance rhythm, camera path, edit structure or scene. Describe the important observed action sequence rather than relying solely on “exactly follow the video.” Do not fabricate cadence from missing evidence or prolong the source simply because the output allows more time; honor the requested duration and continuation policy.

## Build a continuous, visible action phrase

Keep connected gestures together when they serve one development: preparation → action → visible consequence. The camera should make that development readable. A prop handoff needs an owner after the transfer; a reaction follows its trigger; an intermediate pose need not become a static hold.

Choose framing that shows the relevant action: bodies and floor contact for choreography, both sides of a relationship when needed, or the product surface during its use. Integrate commercial detail into handling, movement and material response when the request calls for a campaign rather than a catalogue.

Use a static camera when it serves observation, tension or a locked layout. For movement, describe a coherent path and the reveal it enables; sequential moves are valid when their cues and direction are clear. A focus change is not a camera translation. Avoid simultaneously calling the camera fixed and moving. Do not add an orbit, shake or push to every shot.

## Make boundaries specific, not louder

At a cut, retain the relevant screen direction, eyeline, object ownership, wardrobe and action state. Do not restart a completed action or silently skip a user-required transformation. Ellipsis is valid when the omitted action is not required and the resulting state is understandable.

An ordinary editorial cut needs no invented optical effect. When a stylized transition is wanted, name the actual bridge: outgoing motion or shape, brief occlusion/blur or transformation, and the incoming view that resolves it. Describe it once at the boundary rather than applying flash, refocus or shake throughout every content shot. Persistent stylistic effects are retained when requested. A UI selection, curtain opening or local material change is not automatically a full-frame transition.

## Allocate time to content

Honor explicit timing and evidenced reference cadence first. Within open time, allow the central action and its readable outcome to happen before budgeting secondary poses, reactions or an ending. Choose shot count from that allocation. Equal durations can serve a beat; unequal durations are not inherently better. Do not use a fixed main-action percentage or mandatory walking/branding template.

Preserve a requested ending or keyframe. Otherwise resolve the current development or leave an appropriate hook; do not invent a logo, title, factual claim or new plot just to end the clip. Avoid long decorative holds that displace the requested performance.

## Keep the final prompt economical

State stable source bindings and global style once in their existing sections. In each shot, write the visible action phrase, necessary framing/camera behavior and changed or continuity-critical details. Repeat a product or appearance anchor only where it materially prevents loss, not every attribute in every paragraph. Keep timing consistent with the caller's target. Editorial notes belong in existing planning fields where supported, not in the executable H3 prose.
